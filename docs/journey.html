The story

One person, three VMs, a switcher at IBC

The spec pages tell you what MXL is. This is what it was like to actually build one — alone, in the cloud, on rented compute, and put it on air during the biggest week in the broadcast calendar.

By Guy Cochran · Office Hours GlobalSeptember 2026

The premise

For as long as anyone reading this has worked in broadcast, the production switcher has been a box: SDI in, SDI out, hardware in the middle, and a price tag to match. The EBU's Dynamic Media Facility asked a different question — what if the switcher were software, and the "wiring" were shared memory? The Media eXchange Layer is the piece that makes it possible: media functions read and write timestamped grains in a common memory domain instead of passing frames down a fixed chain.

I wanted to know if it was real — not in a vendor demo, but under the conditions a real show creates: remote guests, a flaky camera, visitor load, a live audience watching. So I built one.

From one VM to a facility

It started on a single Azure D8s_v5 — 8 cores. Test pattern, file playout, a selector, an HTML5 keyer, WebRTC out. It cut. Then I added a real camera over SRT, then a second, then remote guests bridged across a second VM over the MXL Fabrics API, then a native multiviewer composited inside the domain, then a program audio mixer, then a live recording landing in a time-addressable store I could clip from mid-show.

Each addition taught the box a new way to run out of cores. 8 became 16, 16 became 32 cores and 128 GB of RAM for the production node, with two smaller VMs carrying the fabric receiver and the TAMS store. A three-machine, cross-host software facility — and the whole thing billed at $2.11 an hour.

The number that still surprises people

A production facility scaled to 32 cores / 128 GB RAM, plus two supporting VMs, running broadcast-grade live production — for the price of a coffee per hour. No hardware rack. No SDI router. Just cloud compute you can turn off when you're done.

Everything that broke

This is the honest part, and the valuable part. Remote cameras stuttered the instant I cut to them — because MXL indexes grains by time, and a remote feed's grains arrive late. Readers wedged silently when a source restarted, and the status API cheerfully reported "running" the whole time. A feed would show a full 30 frames a second of the same frame and every health metric stayed green. Self-healing watchdogs, each sensible alone, once ganged up into a 38-restart storm visible on the live program.

I wrote all of it down — the timing model, the five-species reader-lifecycle bug taxonomy, the measurement doctrine that finally told me when a feed was actually good and not just moving. That document is the thing I'm proudest of, because it's the part nobody else could have written without doing it.

IBC week

I stood the public domain up on September 13, 2026 — mid-IBC — and put the switcher on air. In its first week the edge served 10.5 GB of live video through the browser and reached viewers in 15 countries, including the Netherlands, IBC's own floor. The same week, MXL won the IBC 2026 Innovation Award for Content Creation, and Grass Valley, Matrox, Riedel, Evertz and others showed it at vendor scale. I wasn't the only one — I was just the one doing it alone, in public, with the receipts posted. See the field →

Why it's dark now — and still here

The live VMs are deallocated; running a 32-core facility 24/7 for a demo isn't the point. But the switcher was built so the story survives the compute: this site, the findings, the explainer and a recorded run stand on their own with no VMs at all. That's the last lesson — a software facility can be turned off and turned back on, and the knowledge doesn't evaporate with the instance.

That's the whole idea, really. Broadcast production stops being a thing you own and becomes a thing you compose — when you need it, where you need it, for what it costs to rent. Here's why I think that's the future →
